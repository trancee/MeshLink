MeshLink routing simulation — stage D: closing the gaps

Every number on this page is simulated. It comes from a simplified computer model of phones and Bluetooth links, calibrated against a few lab measurements. It is not a measurement on real devices.

The question

Stage C chose an adaptive v1 configuration: each phone estimates how many phones are around it and picks low, medium or high settings on its own. Three gaps were left. Stage D tests targeted fixes on top of the stage C v1 configuration (relay preference off, since it was dropped from v1):

  1. Slow unicast at medium and high density. The 95th-percentile delivery time (p95) for one-to-one messages was 2–3 s; the aim is ≤ 1 s. Tested: keeping routes alive while two phones talk (K1), refreshing routes when a delivery ack comes back (K2), keeping the reverse path cached (K3), and a faster route search for distant phones (F).
  2. Phones that are actually alone but classify as medium. A few medium-density cells lost 0.5–1 pp for senders who were really in a sparse spot. Tested: a phone with fewer than 5 phones nearby (its own estimate) always floods its own messages (S5).
  3. Broadcast in a large half-iOS mesh. At N = 1000 with half the phones on iOS (often asleep), broadcast at medium density stayed just under 99%. Tested: a shorter repair timeout (FG) and a catch-up announcement when a link comes up (CU).

Also shown: the pinned low class preset (every phone uses the low-density settings — flood everything — whatever its density), as a low-latency reference. Criteria are those of stage C, applied by the sender’s true local density at send: delivery within 0.5 pp of flooding (and ≥ 99% where flooding reaches it) at low density, ≥ 99% at medium, ≥ 95% at high; first-message p95 ≤ 1 s (≤ 1.5 s post-quantum); idle control traffic ≤ 10% of capacity; then the lowest busiest-phone load, then the lowest p95.

360 simulation runs in the main set. Tables show the mean over seeds and a 95% confidence interval (±). Unless stated otherwise: N = 200 phones, lifetime 5 min, minimum hold 10 s, clock skew ±30 s, identifiers rotate every 15 min, adaptive topology (6 links at low, 5 at medium and high). All protocol variants of one run share the same simulated phones, movements and links, so differences between variants are paired.

What we found

All numbers below are simulated (N = 200 unless stated; mean over seeds, ± = 95% confidence interval).

Recommended v1 change from stage C: unicast adds F (with no route, search the whole mesh at once, alongside the short 3-hop flood) and K2 (the recipient sends a delivery ack along the route, which refreshes the route; with no ack the sender re-sends once by a new search). Broadcast adds CU (catch-up: on every new link, both sides announce the messages they received in the last 60 s, and the other side pulls what it lacks). Everything else stays as in stage C, with relay preference off.

  • Criteria. Stage D v1 (UA F+K2 · BA CU) meets every criterion in 16/16 dynamic cells and 37/40 fixed-density cells. Stage C adaptive meets them in 15/16 and 35/40, and flooding (U1 · B1 on the same topology) in 16/16 and 36/40. The 3 remaining v1 failures are small samples. Two are one lost message each from a truly sparse sender inside a medium cell: about 11–14 such messages per run, 5 seeds, CI ±4–4.6 pp. The third is medium-class senders inside a high cell at 98.06% (about 20 messages per run). The busiest phone carries 1,113 B/s on average (stage C 1,101, flooding 1,849).
  • Unicast p95 ≤ 1 s at medium and high: not reached everywhere. F is the change that matters. Averaged over cells, p95 for medium senders falls from 3,628 to 1,262 ms and for high senders from 2,423 to 983 ms (v1). v1 meets ≤ 1 s in 30 of 56 cells (stage C: 8). Route keeping alone (K1, K2, K3 without F) barely helps: with standard traffic a pair writes about every 12 min, so routes expire anyway, and the p95 comes from first contact with a distant recipient (more than 3 hops). K3 (reverse-path cache) and the full combination F+K1+K2+K3 reach ≤ 1 s in 41–42 of 56 cells. They also roughly double the busiest phone's load (about 2,000 B/s, more than flooding's 1,849) and control traffic (126 → 256–275 B/node·s). With conversation traffic (replies within about 30 s), F reaches ≤ 1 s in 14 of 20 cells and F+K3 in 18 of 20. Long chains stay slow: medium corridor at N = 200 has a p95 of about 7.4 s with every routed variant.
  • Sparse sender floods (S5): does not close the gaps. S5 changed nothing measurable. In the two medium cells that still fail (group half-iOS, walking Android), the lost message comes from a sender whose own estimate was not below 5. Even S8 and S10 did not recover it in a re-run of those two runs. The third stage C cell (medium corridor Android) now passes for every variant. The sparse-corridor gap at 120 s lifetime and 60 s hold is closed, but by F, not S5: UA 98.54%, UA S5 98.55%, S5+F+K1 99.00% against U1 99.00% (same runs). In the follow-up runs, F+K2 reached 99.19% against U1 98.97%. Recommendation: leave S5 out of v1.
  • N = 1000 half-iOS arrival, broadcast: closed by catch-up. At medium density the shorter graft timeout (FG) stays at 98.88% ± 1.70, the same as without it (98.87%) and as pure flooding (B1, 98.88%). The losses come from background iOS phones that sleep through a message, not from slow tree repair. Catch-up reaches 99.99% ± 0.02 for medium senders (99.99% overall). It costs about 10 B/node·s of control traffic and raises the busiest phone's broadcast load from 42 to 114 B/s. At N = 200 catch-up also lifts broadcast to 100.00% in every cell group.
  • N = 1000 unicast: v1 (F+K2) delivers 99.81% (UA 99.44%, same seeds). p95 for medium senders falls from 9.7 to 6.3 s and for high senders from 9.6 to 6.7 s, still far above 1 s. Control traffic rises from 696 to 1,039 B/node·s; the busiest phone goes from 5,758 to 5,943 B/s. Idle control traffic stays below 0.1% of capacity in every set.
  • Pinned low class (low-latency preset). It meets every criterion in all 16 dynamic cells. Unicast p95 is about 0.35–0.62 s and medium and high senders stay at or under about 0.43 s. The busiest phone carries 2.3–3.8 KB/s, about 2–4× v1 outside the low-density standing cells. The radio power index is 1.0, against 0.61–0.96 for v1 (an assumed index, not a battery measurement).
  • iPhones keep at most 4 incoming links: v1 still holds. In the half-iOS recheck (medium N = 50 and 200, high N = 200, arrival N = 200, 5 seeds), v1 and flooding meet every criterion in all 20 combinations of cell and calibration. With degree 6 / 5 / 5 and one slot kept free for newcomers, an iPhone holds about 3.1–4.2 links on average, of which about 1.5–2.0 are incoming. The cap of 4 rarely binds: full iPhones refuse 0.17–0.34 dials per phone per minute. Unicast delivery moves by at most 0.2 pp (lowest 99.65%), broadcast stays ≥ 99.98%, and the busiest phone stays below flooding's in every cell (v1 607–1,074 B/s against flooding 1,485–2,488 B/s). No fix is needed. The cheapest hygiene rule is that an iPhone stops advertising while it holds 4 incoming links. It cuts refused dials by about 25–40% with no measurable change in delivery.
  • Bake-off calibration (recheck only). The device measurements changed four model inputs: per-platform throughput and RTT (an iPhone dialer gets 19–31 KB/s), GATT set-up 1.3–3.7 s, the PQ handshake 101–255 ms, and up to 3 in-dial retries. Under these values, delivery is unchanged, but routes are set up more slowly. Unicast p95 rises from 984 to 1,563 ms for high senders at high density and from 922 to 1,652 ms in the arrival scenario, so the ≤ 1 s aim is met in fewer cells. First-message p95 (post-quantum) stays ≤ 890 ms, inside the 1.5 s criterion. The main stage D grid was not re-run with the new calibration.

1. Faster unicast at medium and high density

Variants (each on top of stage C adaptive unicast, UA): K1 keeps a route alive for 3 min after the last message between the pair; K2 the recipient sends a small delivery ack back along the route, which refreshes the route at every hop (and, if the ack does not arrive in time, the sender re-sends once by a new route search); K3 every phone on the way keeps the reverse path to the sender for 3 min; F when no route is known, the route search goes out to the whole mesh at once, alongside the short 3-hop flood of the message itself, instead of widening step by step (1, 3, 5, 7 hops, then everyone). Near = recipient 1–3 hops away, far = more than 3 hops. “Repeat” = messages to a recipient the sender has written to before. Standard traffic: each phone writes to 3 contacts, one message every 4 min on average, so a given pair talks about every 12 min and routes usually expire in between. Conversation traffic: the recipient replies with probability 0.6 after 30 s on average (chains continue), the case route keeping is designed for.
