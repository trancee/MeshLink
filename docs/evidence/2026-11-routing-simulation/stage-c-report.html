MeshLink routing simulation — stage C: adapting to local, changing density

Every number on this page is simulated. It comes from a simplified computer model of phones and Bluetooth links, calibrated against a few lab measurements. It is not a measurement on real devices.

The question

Stages A and B chose how phones connect (N1+N2, about 5 links each) and compared ways to move messages. They held one crowd density for a whole run. Real crowds change: people walk into a festival (few phones nearby → hundreds), leave again, or stand at the dense center while others are at the sparse edge. Stage C asks: can each phone measure its own surroundings and adapt on its own, and does that beat one fixed setting for everyone?

  • Each phone counts the distinct phones it heard in recent Bluetooth scans (plus its own links), smooths the count and places itself in a class: low (under ~10 phones in radio range), medium (10–50) or high (50+). There is no voting or coordination between phones.
  • To avoid flapping, a phone switches up and down at different thresholds (hysteresis) and stays in a class for a minimum time (dwell).
  • Adapting only changes the phone’s own decisions (how it forwards, how many links it keeps, how often it scans). The messages on the air are the same in every mode, and every phone relays every message type, so phones in different modes interoperate.

Per scenario, three options are compared: (i) the adaptive policy, (ii) the best single fixed configuration (one setting for every phone and every density), and (iii) flooding everywhere (U1 + B1, the most robust and most expensive option). The criteria are applied by the sender’s true local density at the moment of sending: delivery within 0.5 pp of flooding (and ≥ 99% where flooding reaches it) at low density, ≥ 99% at medium, ≥ 95% at high; first-message p95 ≤ 1 s (≤ 1.5 s post-quantum); idle control traffic ≤ 10% of capacity; then the lowest busiest-phone load, then the lowest p95 latency.

2640 simulation runs in the main set. Tables show the mean over seeds and a 95% confidence interval (±). Unless stated otherwise: N = 200 phones, lifetime 5 min, minimum hold 10 s, clock skew ±30 s, identifiers rotate every 15 min. All protocol variants of one run share the same simulated phones, movements and links.

What we found

All numbers below are simulated (model, not devices).

  1. A phone can judge its own density well enough. Counting the distinct phones heard in the last 10 s (plus linked phones not heard) and smoothing it (w10e) gives 0.89–1.20× the true number of peers in range, with 8–27% average error. Longer windows over-count badly (60 s: 1.1–2.8×) because walking phones pass through range. Identifier rotation every 15 min adds about 1% (window ÷ rotation period); even if every phone rotated at the same instant the switch rate did not change measurably.
  2. Hysteresis stops flapping. With up-thresholds at 12.5 and 62.5 and down-thresholds at 8 and 40 (band 25%) and a 60 s minimum stay, a phone standing at a boundary switches 0.6–1.8 times per hour (target: a few). Walking phones switch 18–28 times per hour, but the true class itself changes about 31–34 times per hour for them. A phone follows a real change after 45–57 s (p90).
  3. Mixing modes works. Phones in different modes relay each other’s messages with no change on the air. Unicast across the mode boundary arrived 99.0–100% in every dynamic scenario, broadcast 99.3–99.9%; during transitions 99.4–99.8%.
  4. The adaptive policy meets every criterion in all 16 dynamic scenario cells (arrival, departure, core+edge, surge, boundary), as does flooding. Against flooding everywhere it cuts the busiest phone’s load by 22–78% in the dynamic scenarios (−37% averaged over all cells) and radio-on time by 3–44%. It costs latency: unicast p95 is 2.3–3.6 s instead of 0.4–0.7 s (first messages still arrive in ~0.12 s at p95). Exceptions: phones standing at the low/medium boundary and in fixed low-density cells carry 30–35% more than with flooding on d = 5, because the low class keeps 6 links.
  5. Fixed-density cells: the adaptive policy passes 34 of 40 cells, flooding 38. Flooding itself fails 2 sparse corridor cells (medium-density senders inside a sparse corridor stay under 99%). The other 4 adaptive failures each come from 1–2 lost messages among about 150–190 in a sub-group (true low-density senders in a medium-density cell, which the estimator places in medium; one post-quantum first message that needed a retry).
  6. Class boundaries. Non-flooding unicast (U6, U4sf) and Plumtree (B3) fall below the criteria only when the sender has fewer than 5 peers in range; from 5 peers up they pass. No protocol fails above 10 peers. So the 10-peer boundary has a 2× safety margin for the estimator error, and the 50-peer boundary is not set by delivery (nothing fails on either side); it is set by load and radio time.
  7. Relay preference for awake phones does not help in this model: delivery +0.00 ± 0.02 pp, first-message p95 +29 ± 13 ms, busiest phone +4%. Recommend leaving it out.
  8. Minimum message lifetime 120 s. With ±30 s clock skew, 60 s lifetimes cost the adaptive policy 0.4–2.0 pp of delivery (worst in sparse corridors) while flooding is unaffected. At 120 s the adaptive policy stays within 0.5 pp of flooding in every tested cell. Minimum hold 0 s and 10 s are indistinguishable; 60 s hurts short lifetimes.
  9. Store-and-forward pays off over long lifetimes. In 1-hour runs at low density, holding raised delivery including destinations that were unreachable at send time from 43% to 84% (corridor, Android) and from 63% to 96% (walking, half iOS). 1 h and 24 h lifetimes gave the same result within an hour.
  10. Tag rotation every 15 min is within budget in every density class: data transmissions −0.2 to +0.1%, control +0.2 to +2.7%, delivery change within ±0.03 pp (budget: +10% transmissions, −1 pp).
  11. Bridge rule rate limit 60 s: partition repair 6.8 s (low), 6.2 s (medium), 7.0 s (high) vs 11.3 / 14.2 / 26 s without the rule, for +0 / +2 / +3% link setups.
  12. 6 links at low density are worth keeping: the follow-up with 5 links everywhere cut low-density load by 12–19% but shrank the connected group by 1.1–1.4 pp and lost 0.8 ± 0.3 pp of all messages at low density, N = 200.

Words used here

  • Peers in range: other phones close enough to hear over Bluetooth. Density classes: low < 10, medium 10–49, high ≥ 50.
  • U1: managed flood (every phone relays every message once). U4sf: on-demand route search (AODV-like) with tuned store-and-forward. U6: hybrid; a short flood (3 hops) carries the message while the route is found, then the route is used. UA: adaptive unicast; a phone in the low class floods, in medium and high it behaves as U6. UA4: low floods, medium U6, high U4.
  • B1: broadcast flood. B3: Plumtree (payload along a self-repairing tree). BA: adaptive broadcast; B1 behavior in the low class, B3 in medium and high, same messages on the air.
  • fixed-d5 / fixed-d6: N1+N2 topology with 5 or 6 links per phone, bridge rule with the chosen rate limit. adaptive: 6 links at low, 5 at medium and high, plus the per-class scan and advertising duty. adaptive-noduty: the same without the duty change.
  • +pref: iOS-aware relay preference. +rot: routing tag rotates every 15 minutes.
  • Message lifetime, minimum hold, store-and-forward, hop limit: as in the project glossary.
