MeshLink routing simulation — stage E: what do the security features cost?

Every number on this page is simulated. It comes from a simplified computer model of phones and Bluetooth links, calibrated against lab measurements (bearer bake-off, 2026-11). It is not a measurement on real devices.

Stages A–D chose the v1 routing configuration. Stage E measures what its privacy and security features cost in delivery, delay and bytes: (1) how often phones change their identifiers, (2) how long the routing tag and message ID must be, (3) how relays handle messages longer than one Bluetooth packet, and (4) the post-quantum link handshake when links come and go often. Baseline: final v1 (stage D v1 with iPhones keeping at most 3 incoming links), bake-off calibration. Cells: 200 phones at low, medium and high density standing still, medium density walking, and the arrival scenario (a crowd converging on a venue over 10 min); each in an Android-only and a half-iPhone mix; 5 seeds each.

All numbers below are simulated (N = 200, 10 cells: low, medium and high static, medium walking and arrival, each Android-only and half iOS; 5 seeds; ± = 95% confidence interval). Baseline: final v1 (stage D v1 + iOS inbound cap 3, iPhone at the cap not discoverable), bake-off calibration. Wall time about 41 min (pilot 7 min + 2,048 s). Nothing was cut.

  • Tag rotation: recommend 5 min. It is the shortest period clearly within the budget (≤ +10% transmissions, ≤ −1 pp delivery) in every density class: worst −0.26 pp delivery and +1.4% bytes. 1 min is within on delivery (worst −0.25 pp) but its bytes on air are unclear in the arrival half-iOS cell (+10.8 ± 1.5%; data transmissions only +1.0%; the extra bytes are route searches), route-search bytes rise 60–109% at low density, and the density estimator over-counts by 12–13% at low density. 15 min (old v1) and 60 min are within. Aligned 5 min (every phone rotates at once) is also within (+3.1% bytes). With a 5 min period the 10 s estimator window is 1/30 of the period and the over-count is 2.5% (low), 1.3% (medium), 0.6% (high).
  • Routing tag: keep 8 B. 4 B saves 6–7% of unicast routing bytes but the whole-mesh collision probability at N = 1000 is 1.0×10-3 per rotation period (0.10 per day at 15 min). 8 B is 2.4×10-13 per period. 16 B costs +11% unicast bytes for no measurable gain.
  • Message ID: recommend 12 B. It saves 5.2% of broadcast routing bytes against 16 B, and random collisions stay negligible (8.4×10-19 per 24 h lifetime at N = 1000). 8 B saves 10.5%, and its random-collision probability is small per mesh (2.6×10-6 per year at the 24 h maximum lifetime), but across a million meshes that is a few lost messages per year. Delivery did not change with any size (|Δ| ≤ 0.03 pp). Deliberate ID or tag reuse by a malicious phone is a threat-model question at any size: bind the ID to the content.
  • Long messages: keep per-hop reassembly. Store-then-forward loses at most 0.15 pp (16 × 514 B) against single-fragment messages. Cut-through cuts 3-hop median latency (774 → 435 ms at 16 × 514 B) but loses 2.48 pp of unicast delivery and 3.9 pp of broadcast delivery under churn, down to 92.5% in half-iOS cells. The cut-through model is pessimistic (see caveats), so this is an upper bound on its loss, not a proof that it cannot work.
  • XXhfs (post-quantum) under churn: within budget. Handshakes use 0.12–0.41% of link capacity with XXhfs (0.01–0.03% with XX). Delivery does not change. Dial-to-usable p50 rises by about 0.1 s (2.8–3.5 s), dominated by GATT setup. First-message p95 (median over seeds) stays below 1 s classical and 1.5 s post-quantum for 1–3 hops in every cell and density class; worst is 828 ms (PQ, 3 hops, high static half iOS). Pooled over all cells, 99.8% of first messages are within budget (classical IK 99.8%, PQ IK 99.9%). The rare misses are single messages that hit a 60 s iPhone background gap.

Caveats. Rotation changes only advertised identifiers; peers are still chosen by true identity, so rotation reaches the topology only through the density estimator. Paired rotation runs diverge chaotically; the 60 min row (only ~25% of phones rotate once in 15 min) shows the noise floor of about ±3.5% bytes. Cut-through keeps a broken partial copy in the seen set (a later full copy is dropped), acks at head time and holds the link while waiting. Fragment size is MTU − 3; the measured throughput is assumed at MTU 185 too. Handshake airtime gets only the contention factor. Collision figures are analytic and random-only.

1. How often should a phone change its identifiers?

Each phone advertises a short-lived identifier and is addressed by a short-lived routing tag. Both change together every rotation period, so an observer cannot follow a phone for long. A change has a cost: neighbors briefly see the phone as a new one (the local density estimate counts it twice), and routes to the old tag are useless, so the mesh must find the phone again. Each phone rotates at its own random moment (its phase), except in the aligned row where all phones rotate at the same instant. Tags follow the phone's own clock (±30 s skew); a receiver accepts the previous, current and next tag.

Budget (accepted design): at most +10% transmissions and at most −1 percentage point delivery against no rotation, judged per density class. "Against no rotation" compares each run with the same cell and seed run without any rotation (paired), so it includes every knock-on effect on links and the density estimate. Bytes counts all routing and link-control bytes on the air (unicast and broadcast data, route search, neighbor lists); transmissions counts data frames sent. "Same run, tags fixed" compares with a copy of the unicast protocol inside the same run whose tags never change (the topology is then identical), which isolates the cost of relearning routes.

"Per year" is the chance of at least one collision anywhere in the mesh during a year of this traffic (expected collisions ≈ rate · m / 2b per second).

3. Long messages: reassemble at every hop or pass fragments straight on?

A BLE link carries at most one ATT payload per write: 182 B at MTU 185 or 514 B at MTU 517. Longer messages are split into fragments. With store, then forward (per-hop reassembly) a relay waits for the whole message, checks it, and only then sends it on; each hop adds the full message's air time. With cut-through a relay passes each fragment on as soon as it arrives; only the first hop pays the full air time. Every message here is exactly k fragments on the wire. Link throughput is the measured 47–79 KB/s (Android dials) or 19–31 KB/s (iPhone dials) at both MTUs (the bake-off measured throughput at MTU 517 only; assumed). Cut-through is modeled pessimistically: if a link drops mid-message, the partial copy downstream is lost and a later complete copy of the same message is still dropped as a duplicate.

4. Post-quantum link handshake under churn

Every new link starts with a Noise handshake. v1 uses the post-quantum XXhfs (about 2.5 KB, 101–255 ms measured) instead of classical XX (about 200 B, 55–90 ms). Phones that walk around or arrive at a venue make many new links, so the handshake cost grows with churn. The extra air time of the larger handshake is modeled on top of the measured handshake time, slowed by the same crowd factor as data. Separately, the first message between two phones carries an end-to-end Noise IK message: about 96 B classical or about 2.4 KB post-quantum. Half the pairs in each run use each kind.

Handshake share: handshake bytes divided by the capacity of all live links over the same time. Dial to usable: from the decision to dial until the authenticated link can carry data (includes retries and the GATT set-up of 1.3–3.7 s).
